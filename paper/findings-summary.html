The 61% Problem

I measured how accurately AI reads real Pakistani customer complaints. It is right about 61% of the time — not the 95% that standard testing reports.

What I did

I collected every public review from Pakistan's main mobile wallets and telecom apps — JazzCash, Easypaisa, SadaPay, NayaPay, Zindigi and others. Real complaints, written by real customers, in Urdu, Roman Urdu, English, and the mix of all three that people actually type.

I then asked leading AI models to sort those complaints into categories — the sort of thing a bank does automatically before routing a ticket to the right team. Finally, I read 365 of those complaints myself and decided the correct answer for each one, without ever seeing what the AI had said.

That last step is the one almost nobody does, and it is the only step that tells you whether the AI is right.

The main finding

Tested the usual way

95%

Accuracy when the test questions were written in-house — clean, clear, one problem each. This is how most teams check their systems.

Tested on real complaints

61%

Accuracy on genuine customer reviews from the same apps. Same AI, same categories, same instructions. Only the questions changed.

A 34-point gap. Writing your own test questions is like a teacher sitting their own exam — you unconsciously write the questions you know how to answer. Real customers write "bakwas app", ramble about three problems at once, switch scripts mid-sentence, and end with fifteen question marks.

If your AI is graded on questions your team wrote, you should expect the real number to be roughly thirty points lower.

Three more things worth knowing

31%

When two different AI models agree with each other, they are still both wrong about a third of the time.

Many teams use this as a shortcut: run two models, and where they agree, trust the answer. On real Pakistani complaints that shortcut fails roughly one time in three — and it fails invisibly, because agreement looks like confidence.

17 : 1

Keyword rules miss Urdu-script complaints seventeen times more often than English ones.

I built a keyword search to find complaints where a customer had lost money. It missed 17 Urdu-script cases for every 1 English case it missed — because the keyword list was written in English letters. Any rule built this way is close to blind to customers writing in Urdu.

Some kinds of fraud never appear in public reviews at all.

Scams where a customer is deceived by another person — a forged payment screenshot shown to a shopkeeper — returned zero examples across the entire corpus. The app worked correctly, so there is nothing to complain to the app about. That victim goes to the police. Any system trained only on public feedback is blind to this entire category.

These are different problems with different owners. JazzCash's unhappy customers are mostly describing a product they find hard to use. Easypaisa's are mostly describing a service that was unavailable. One is a design problem; the other is infrastructure.

Money-loss complaints are near-identical between them, and the difference is inside the margin of error. Neither wallet is meaningfully worse at losing people's money.

Why this matters commercially

Pakistan's central bank requires every bank and wallet to run a Complaint Management Unit, and puts a 30 working-day limit on resolving fraud complaints. At that volume, no one is sorting complaints by hand.

If the system doing the sorting is 61% accurate rather than 95%, roughly four complaints in ten land in the wrong queue. Some of those are fraud cases, sitting in the wrong place while a regulatory clock runs down — and nobody is looking, because the dashboard says 95%.

What this study cannot tell you

  • One person decided the correct answers. There was no second reader, so "61% accurate" is more precisely "61% agreement with one experienced reader."
  • I could not detect small differences between languages. The study would reliably have caught a gap of 28 percentage points or more between Urdu and English. It found around 11 — statistically indistinguishable from chance. A smaller real gap could exist.
  • The corpus is 88% English. That is what Pakistanis actually write in app stores, but it means the Urdu findings rest on a smaller base.
  • Public reviews are not customer service tickets. They capture the people who gave up and complained publicly, which is a different population from the people who filed a formal complaint.

Every one of those limits is stated because it is real. A study that reports only its strengths should be read as marketing.
